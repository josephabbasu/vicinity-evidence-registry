## 1. Major Themes

### 1.1 Ambient contextual crime vs. personally reported exposure are empirically non-equivalent

This is the attached manuscript's own central claim, and the field literature both motivates and only partially resolves it. Chen, Corvo, Lee, and Hahm (2017) used four waves of Add Health and found that witnessed violence tracked more closely with concurrent adolescent depressive symptoms while direct victimization was more strongly related to later depression. Latzman and Swisher (2005) linked street violence and adolescents' own violence involvement to depression across two Add Health waves. No study identified in this search directly compares an ambient administrative crime measure against personally reported exposure within the same cohort and model — which is precisely the gap the attached manuscript fills.

The companion systematic review reinforces this distinction from the causal-inference side: it explicitly separates "core" studies that directly measure community violence from "adjacent" studies that measure related-but-non-equivalent exposures (bullying, intimate partner violence, family firearm injury), and it finds that exposure precision (geocoded blocks/days vs. counties/years) is one of the three features that most explains variation in detectable effects across the corpus.

### 1.2 Theoretical frameworks: stress-process, ecological, and life-course perspectives

As in the attached manuscript, three traditions frame the field without requiring exposure-measure equivalence:
- **Stress-process theory** (Pearlin, Menaghan, Lieberman, & Mullan, 1981; Pearlin, 1989) situates psychological strain within recurring stressors and coping resources.
- **Ecological/bioecological models** (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006; Leventhal & Brooks-Gunn, 2000) locate neighborhood conditions within nested family, peer, school, and institutional systems.
- **Life-course theory** (Elder, 1998) motivates modeling first occurrence, recurrence, and persistence as developmentally distinct.

The companion systematic review adds a fourth organizing idea specific to the causal-inference literature: a hypothesized **three-system developmental sequence** — arousal/vigilance, cognitive control/school functioning, and longer-term psychopathology — explicitly framed as a testable hypothesis generated by its synthesis, not a demonstrated pathway.

### 1.3 Developmental timing and exposure course

Beyond Add Health-specific timing work (Kim, 2018, on firearm access; Russell, Vasilenko, & Lanza, 2016, on age-varying associations), the companion systematic review's incident-proximal literature shows that severe, recent, geographically proximal violent events are followed by measurable short-run changes in sleep, attention, academic performance, school attendance, and mental health service use (e.g., Heissel et al., 2018, on sleep/cortisol; Vasan et al., 2021, on emergency-department mental health visits with a 14-day peak and detectable signal to 60 days). This is a distinct evidentiary strand from Add Health's within-person and sibling exposure-course models — it identifies the *front end* of a possible developmental sequence that the attached manuscript's recurrent/post-exposure findings may represent the *back end* of, an integration neither attached manuscript makes explicit but that the two documents jointly support.

### 1.4 Family- and genetically informed designs

Add Health-specific work in this vein has expanded since the attached manuscript's own reference list. Jaffee, Hasford, and Fein (2023) used sibling fixed effects (baseline N = 20,745; outcomes at ages 24–32) and found that each additional gun/knife-violence exposure was associated with more adult depressive symptoms (b = 0.12, 95% CI 0.05–0.19) and 7% higher odds of clinically significant depression. A newly identified study, **Kim, J., & Kim, T. (2025)**, used an Add Health sibling subsample (N = 1,474, Waves I–V) with sibling fixed effects and found that adolescent dating-violence victimization was significantly associated with higher adult depressive symptoms even after controlling for shared family background, with later violence involvement and sleep problems mediating about 25% of the effect. Outside Add Health, the companion systematic review's adjacent-evidence section catalogs four other genetically informed designs — Schaefer et al. (2018, discordant twins, UK), Connolly et al. (2022, twin design, intimate partner victimization), Singham et al. (2017, MZ/DZ twin-differences, bullying), and Pitkänen et al. (2026, Finnish sibling comparison, registry violent victimization) — that collectively show victimization-psychopathology associations surviving strong familial controls across multiple national samples, strengthening (without substituting for) the attached manuscript's own sibling and monozygotic-twin findings.

### 1.5 Physiological and biological embedding

Ford and Browning (2014) remains the closest Add Health precedent for a cardiovascular outcome: adolescents who witnessed weapon violence (males) or were victimized with a weapon (females) around Wave I had increased odds of adulthood hypertension. No Add Health study of allostatic load specifically was identified in this search — the allostatic-load literature is dominated by non-Add-Health work, most notably **Gresham et al. (2025)**, a systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizing 36 studies on community violence exposure and allostatic-load mediators (cortisol, epinephrine, norepinephrine, dehydroepiandrosterone). **Finegood et al. (2024)** — cited in the attached manuscript without a stated sample — is confirmed by this search to use a six-wave panel of 157 **female** adolescents and young adults (a Canadian sample per the companion systematic review's Table 1), not Add Health; it found increased monocyte reactivity to stimulation and decreased glucocorticoid sensitivity after violence exposure, but no significant change in circulating IL-6 or CRP. This is a correction worth carrying into any submission that cites Finegood et al. as general biomarker evidence: it should not be described as an Add Health study or generalized beyond a small, all-female sample.

### 1.6 Gender-differentiated and asymmetric effects

A newly identified Add Health study, **Kim, J., & Park, K. (2025)**, used an asymmetric fixed-effects design and found that conventional fixed-effects models show violent victimization positively associated with depressive symptoms overall, but that for women the mental-health benefit of *decreases* in victimization exceeded the harm of *increases*, while men showed no such asymmetry. This is a methodological and substantive extension neither attached manuscript engages with: it suggests that pooled fixed-effects coefficients (of the kind reported throughout the attached empirical manuscript) may average over a gendered asymmetry that a single linear coefficient cannot detect.

### 1.7 Recurrence and escalation: does adolescent exposure predict adult victimization?

A newly identified Add Health study, **Roberts, Bushover, Rajan, Aiello, Dodington, and Morrison (2026)**, used Add Health Waves I–III (1994–2002) and structural equation path analysis to test whether adolescent violence and weapon exposure predicts firearm-violence victimization in early adulthood. This reframes exposure as a risk factor for *future exposure* rather than only for depression or cardiovascular outcomes — a pathway the attached manuscript does not model but that is conceptually adjacent to its "post-exposure" transition state, since both concern what happens after an exposure episode.

### 1.9 The broader causal-inference field beyond Add Health

The companion systematic review supplies a comprehensive, independently screened (1,820 records → 33 retained studies) picture of the non-Add-Health causal-inference literature. Its central conclusions, directly relevant to interpreting the attached manuscript's own findings:
- The best-identified evidence in the *broader* field concerns short-run functional disruption (sleep, attention, academic performance, attendance, service use) after discrete, severe, proximal violent events — not psychiatric diagnosis per se.
- Repeated-exposure designs (the type the attached manuscript uses) are associated with internalizing/externalizing symptoms and educational impairment, but the companion review rates this evidence as more vulnerable to time-varying and nonshared confounding than incident-proximal evidence — a caution that applies with equal force to the attached manuscript's own individual and sibling fixed-effects depression models.
- Only one of the companion review's 33 studies (Jaffee et al., 2023) used Add Health, meaning the attached manuscript's Add Health-based approach is complementary to, not redundant with, the broader quasi-experimental literature, most of which relies on geocoded incident data or twin/sibling registries outside Add Health.

## 2. Commonly Used Methods Across the Field

- **Within-person and sibling/twin fixed effects** on panel data — the attached manuscript's own primary method, shared with Jaffee et al. (2023), Kim, J. & Kim, T. (2025), Kim, J. & Park, K. (2025), and, outside Add Health, Schaefer et al. (2018), Connolly et al. (2022), Singham et al. (2017), and Pitkänen et al. (2026).
- **Natural experiments and event-study/difference-in-differences designs** anchored to a discrete violent incident (geocoded shootings, homicides, school shootings) — the dominant design family in the companion systematic review, essentially absent from the Add Health literature because Add Health was not built around dated, geocoded incidents.
- **Structural equation / path analysis** — used by Roberts et al. (2026) to link adolescent exposure to adult victimization.
- **Systematic review and meta-analysis** — used by the companion review itself and by Gresham et al. (2025) for allostatic-load biomarkers.
- **Biological/ex vivo assay methods** (cortisol, monocyte stimulation assays, glucocorticoid sensitivity) — used by Finegood et al. (2024) and Heissel et al. (2018), neither of which uses Add Health.

## 3. Key Findings Across the Field

- Personal, self-reported violence exposure is more consistently associated with depressive symptoms than ambient/contextual crime measures across both the Add Health literature and the attached manuscript's own results.
- Within-family (sibling, twin) designs generally reduce but do not eliminate the exposure–depression association, and the association can be gendered and asymmetric (Kim, J. & Park, K., 2025) or mediated by post-exposure behavior (Kim, J. & Kim, T., 2025).
- Adolescent weapon-specific exposure is linked to later hypertension (Ford & Browning, 2014) and, in the attached manuscript, to elevated blood pressure and mean arterial pressure that partly replicates within siblings.
- Outside Add Health, the strongest causal-inference evidence in the broader field concerns short-run functional and service-use outcomes following discrete violent events, not long-run psychiatric diagnosis — a pattern the attached manuscript's own weaker, more specification-dependent contextual-crime results are broadly consistent with.
- Allostatic-load evidence is comparatively thin and inconsistent across the whole field, in both the attached manuscript (non-replicating sibling estimate) and the broader biomarker literature (Gresham et al., 2025, meta-analysis; Finegood et al., 2024, non-Add-Health, mixed CRP/IL-6 results).

## 4. Disagreements and Unresolved Tensions

- **Whether stronger personal-exposure associations reflect causal proximity or better measurement.** Neither the attached manuscript nor any Add Health study identified here can separate these explanations, because no study has modeled contextual and personal exposure jointly with a common measurement-quality benchmark.
- **Whether within-family designs "replicate" or represent a different, smaller true effect.** The companion review notes this explicitly for area-level vs. individual designs; the attached manuscript's own imprecise MZ-twin estimates and the field's discordant-twin evidence (Schaefer et al., 2018) show the same ambiguity.
- **Whether cardiovascular specificity is real or an artifact of allostatic-load measurement heterogeneity.** This tension, raised explicitly in the attached manuscript, is unresolved by the wider field: Gresham et al.'s (2025) meta-analysis speaks to allostatic load in general but was not identified as testing blood pressure and allostatic load side by side in one design, so it cannot adjudicate the attached manuscript's claim.
- **Whether effects are symmetric.** Kim, J. & Park, K. (2025) is the only source identified that tests for asymmetric (increase vs. decrease) and gendered effects; this raises an open question about whether the attached manuscript's pooled coefficients conceal similar heterogeneity, since its models were not stratified or tested for asymmetry.

## 5. Limitations Across the Field

- Add Health's contextual crime measures remain spatially coarse relative to the geocoded block/day-level exposure now standard in the non-Add-Health causal-inference literature (companion review, Table 1) — a design gap, not just a measurement footnote.
- Self-reported exposure across the Add Health literature is vulnerable to recall bias and shared-method variance with self-reported depression, a concern raised identically in the attached manuscript and in Jaffee et al. (2023) and both new Kim/Kim and Kim/Park studies.
- Sibling and twin designs across the whole field (Add Health and non-Add-Health alike) cannot rule out nonshared, time-varying confounding — stated as a limitation in every family-design study reviewed here.
- The allostatic-load literature suffers from component heterogeneity and small, non-representative samples (Finegood et al.'s 157 female-only participants is a clear example), limiting what can be concluded about multisystem biological embedding.
- Much of the non-Add-Health causal-inference evidence (companion review) is US-concentrated and urban, limiting generalizability — a constraint that does not directly apply to the nationally representative Add Health cohort but does limit how far the broader field's incident-proximal findings can be used to interpret Add Health's own results.

## 6. Unanswered Questions

- Does the personal-exposure signal in Add Health hold up when tested for gendered asymmetry, as Kim, J. & Park, K. (2025) found for general violent victimization?
- Would the attached manuscript's cardiovascular findings replicate using the geocoded, incident-anchored designs common in the non-Add-Health literature, which offer sharper exposure timing than Add Health's wave-based measures?
- Does the "post-exposure" persistence documented in the attached manuscript connect mechanistically to the short-run arousal/vigilance and cognitive-control disruptions documented in the companion review's incident-proximal studies (Heissel et al., 2018; McCoy et al., 2015)? No identified study, Add Health or otherwise, tests this pathway within one design.
- Is the newly identified adolescent-exposure-to-adult-victimization pathway (Roberts et al., 2026) itself confounded by the same personal-exposure processes the attached manuscript studies, and would it persist within siblings?
- Would allostatic load look more cardiovascular-specific, as in the attached manuscript, if assessed with the same component set used in Gresham et al.'s (2025) meta-analysis?

## 7. Synthesis of the Current State of the Field (≈200 words)

The field studying violence exposure, depression, and physiological risk has matured along two largely separate tracks. Outside Add Health, a fast-growing causal-inference literature — cataloged comprehensively in the companion systematic review — uses geocoded, incident-anchored natural experiments to show that severe, proximal violent events reliably disrupt sleep, attention, school functioning, and service use in the short run, with psychiatric outcomes remaining plausible but less securely identified. Inside Add Health, a smaller but active body of work, expanded meaningfully in just the past two years (Kim, J. & Park, K., 2025; Kim, J. & Kim, T., 2025; Roberts et al., 2026), uses within-person, sibling, and now asymmetric fixed-effects designs to link personally reported victimization and witnessing to depression, with newly documented gendered asymmetries and dating-violence pathways that neither attached manuscript currently engages. The attached empirical manuscript occupies a genuinely underserved niche within this landscape: no other identified Add Health study tests contextual crime against personal exposure in the same model, models exposure course as a transition variable, or extends family-based designs to cardiovascular and allostatic-load biomarkers. The field's most persistent open question — whether personal-exposure associations reflect causal proximity or superior measurement — remains unresolved by every source reviewed here, including this manuscript.
