# Structured Literature Review

**Source manuscript:** Abbas, J. *Violence Exposure Is Not a Unitary Construct: Contextual Crime, Personal Exposure, Depression, and Cardiovascular Risk from Adolescence to Midlife* (revised manuscript, prepared for submission to *Social Science & Medicine*).

**Scope note.** This review is built entirely from the 17 works cited in the manuscript's reference list and from how the manuscript itself characterizes each source in its Introduction and Discussion. The original source papers were not separately retrieved or read; where the manuscript does not describe a cited work's dataset, method, or findings in enough detail to summarize accurately, that gap is marked **Not reported** rather than filled in. Two references — Sjölander & Frisell (2019) and Turanovic (2022) — appear in the bibliography but were not found attached to an in-text citation in the extracted manuscript text; they are reviewed here based on their titles/topic and their evident role in the reference list. Goldstein et al. (2019) is treated the same way: it plausibly corresponds to an uncited claim about "mixed longitudinal findings" for neighborhood disadvantage, but no inline citation marker ties it to that sentence in the extracted text, so the linkage is noted as inferred rather than confirmed.

## 1. Major Themes

### 1.1 Contextual crime and personal violence exposure are treated as non-equivalent constructs

The manuscript's central organizing claim — that ambient administrative crime rates, personally reported victimization/witnessing, and accumulated exposure histories are not interchangeable — is built on a set of Add Health studies that each isolated one exposure definition. Chen et al. (2017) separated witnessed and directly experienced community violence across four waves and found that witnessed exposure tracked more closely with *concurrent* adolescent symptoms while direct victimization was more strongly tied to *later* depression. Latzman and Swisher (2005) linked street violence and adolescents' own violence involvement to depression across two waves. Russell et al. (2016) showed that weapon-related violence exposure's association with depressive symptoms varies across age, spanning adolescence into young adulthood. None of these studies directly compared an ambient contextual measure against personal exposure within the same cohort and model — a gap the manuscript identifies explicitly ("It has not resolved whether broad contextual crime and personally encountered violence produce comparable signals when modeled within the same longitudinal cohort") and that motivates its own county-crime-versus-personal-exposure comparison.

### 1.2 Theoretical frameworks: stress-process, ecological, and life-course perspectives

Three theoretical traditions are invoked jointly, rather than as competing explanations, to justify modeling exposure resolution and developmental timing separately:

- **Stress-process theory** (Pearlin, Menaghan, Lieberman, & Mullan, 1981; Pearlin, 1989) situates psychological strain within recurring stressors and the coping resources available to manage them.
- **Ecological/bioecological models** (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006; Leventhal & Brooks-Gunn, 2000) locate neighborhood conditions within nested family, peer, school, and institutional systems, which is the manuscript's basis for treating a county crime rate as an ambient contextual indicator rather than a proxy for personal experience.
- **Life-course theory** (Elder, 1998) supplies the rationale for the manuscript's exposure-course modeling, holding that first occurrence, recurrence, and persistence after exposure may carry distinct developmental implications rather than functioning as a single dose-response gradient.

The manuscript uses this combination to argue that its four exposure operationalizations (contextual, profile, transition, weapon-specific) are theoretically motivated rather than an atheoretical proliferation of measures.

### 1.3 Developmental timing and exposure course

Beyond the general stress-process and life-course framing, two studies speak directly to *timing*: Kim (2018) found that gaining in-home firearm access predicted within-person increases in depressive symptoms, and Russell et al. (2016) documented age-varying associations between violence exposure and health outcomes. The manuscript extends this line by explicitly modeling first-observed, recurrent, and post-exposure transition states rather than a static exposure indicator, reporting the largest coefficient for recurrent exposure and a persistent (though smaller) elevation in the post-exposure state — a pattern it frames as bridging "two literatures that are often separated: studies of changes near violent incidents and studies of repeated exposure across development."

### 1.4 Family- and genetically informed designs for confounding control

Jaffee, Hasford, and Fein (2023) used sibling fixed effects to show that differential weapon-violence exposure between siblings was associated with sibling differences in adult depression — a design intended to remove confounding from shared family conditions. Sjölander and Frisell (2019), a methodological paper on the causal interpretation of sibling-comparison designs, appears in the reference list and is thematically consistent with the manuscript's own cautions about what sibling and monozygotic-twin fixed effects can and cannot rule out (shared family conditions removed; nonshared, time-varying confounding not removed), though the manuscript's extracted text does not show it cited at a specific point. The present manuscript extends the family-design literature by adding a monozygotic-twin layer on top of sibling comparisons, reporting imprecise but positive twin estimates that it explicitly declines to interpret as either replication or refutation.

### 1.5 Physiological and biological embedding of violence exposure

Ford and Browning (2014) linked adolescent weapon exposure to adult hypertension, providing the manuscript's closest precedent for a cardiovascular outcome. Finegood, Weissman-Tsukamoto, Lam, Chen, and Miller (2024) examined interpersonal violence exposure and inflammation during adolescence and young adulthood, and Gresham et al. (2025) — a systematic review and meta-analysis — synthesized evidence on community violence exposure and allostatic-load biomarkers. The manuscript positions its own biomarker analyses (systolic/diastolic/mean arterial pressure and allostatic load) as testing whether this prior work's signal is more consistent for a specific cardiovascular domain or for a broad multisystem index, noting explicitly that "a broad multisystem index can conceal system-specific patterns."

### 1.6 Add Health as a shared analytic platform

Harris (2013) documents the design and accomplishments of the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health), the data source underlying the manuscript and, per the manuscript's own framing, underlying several of its comparison studies (Latzman & Swisher, 2005; Chen et al., 2017; Kim, 2018; Jaffee et al., 2023; Ford & Browning, 2014 — introduced collectively as "Prior Add Health studies"). Turanovic (2022) reflects on 25 years of Add Health violence-exposure and victimization research; it appears in the bibliography but is not tied to a specific in-text claim in the extracted manuscript text, so its precise role (general framing citation vs. a specific claim) is **not reported**.

### 1.7 Neighborhood disadvantage as a contextual measure

The manuscript states that "objective neighborhood disadvantage produces mixed longitudinal findings after individual adjustment," a claim consistent with Goldstein et al. (2019), which examined neighborhood disadvantage and depressive symptoms in adolescents followed into emerging adulthood. No inline citation marker links the two in the extracted text, so this connection should be treated as topically plausible rather than confirmed.

## 2. Commonly Used Methods Across the Reviewed Literature

- **Longitudinal cohort panels**, most often Add Health, used to model within-person change (Chen et al., 2017; Latzman & Swisher, 2005; Kim, 2018; Ford & Browning, 2014; Jaffee et al., 2023; and the present manuscript).
- **Individual and family/sibling fixed-effects estimation**, used to remove stable respondent- or family-level confounding while leaving time-varying confounding unaddressed (Jaffee et al., 2023; present manuscript, extended to monozygotic-twin fixed effects).
- **Systematic review and meta-analysis**, used to aggregate biomarker evidence across primary studies (Gresham et al., 2025) rather than to generate new primary estimates.
- **Theoretical/conceptual synthesis** (no primary data collection), used to frame stress, ecological, and life-course mechanisms rather than to test them directly (Pearlin et al., 1981; Pearlin, 1989; Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006; Leventhal & Brooks-Gunn, 2000; Elder, 1998).

## 3. Key Findings Across the Reviewed Literature

- Witnessed and direct violence exposure show different temporal relationships to adolescent depressive symptoms, with witnessing more concurrent and direct victimization more delayed (Chen et al., 2017).
- Weapon-related exposure is associated with depressive symptoms across a wide age range, with age-varying strength (Russell et al., 2016).
- Firearm access at home predicts within-person increases in depressive symptoms (Kim, 2018).
- Differential sibling exposure to weapon violence is associated with sibling differences in adult depression, supporting a within-family signal net of shared family confounding (Jaffee et al., 2023).
- Adolescent weapon exposure is associated with adult hypertension (Ford & Browning, 2014), and community violence exposure has been linked to inflammatory and allostatic-load biomarkers in a broader synthesis (Finegood et al., 2024; Gresham et al., 2025).
- The present manuscript's own findings — a weak, specification-dependent contextual-crime signal versus a larger and more consistent personal-exposure signal, a recurrent-exposure-largest developmental pattern, sibling replication, and a cardiovascular-specific (rather than broad multisystem) biomarker pattern — are consistent with, and extend, this cited body of work.

## 4. Disagreements and Unresolved Tensions

- **Whether personal-exposure measures are truly more "causally proximal" or simply better measured.** The manuscript itself flags that its own larger personal-exposure coefficients "may therefore reflect greater proximity and salience, but they may also reflect more precise measurement, shared-method variance with self-reported depression, different wave structures, or different within-person variability" — the cited literature does not resolve this because no prior study modeled both measurement types together.
- **Whether an "overlap" (co-occurring victimization and witnessing) profile is statistically distinguishable from single-exposure profiles.** The manuscript notes a suggestive gradient but states that "formal equality tests between coefficients were not available in the evidence table," leaving this an open question rather than an established finding.
- **Whether family-comparison designs replicate or fail to replicate individual-level associations.** Sibling estimates replicated the personal-exposure pattern, but monozygotic-twin estimates were "positive but imprecise," which the manuscript explicitly treats as neither confirming nor refuting the sibling-level findings — an unresolved tension the broader family-design methods literature (e.g., Sjölander & Frisell, 2019, on interpreting sibling comparisons) is directly relevant to but which the manuscript does not resolve.
- **Whether violence exposure biology is cardiovascular-specific or reflects broad multisystem dysregulation.** Blood-pressure associations replicated within siblings; allostatic-load associations were weaker and did not replicate. The manuscript cannot distinguish whether this reflects true physiological specificity or greater measurement heterogeneity in the allostatic-load index — a tension it states outright rather than resolves.

## 5. Limitations Noted Across the Reviewed and Present Literature

- County/administrative crime measures are spatially coarse and cannot establish incident awareness, witnessing, or perceived threat.
- Personal-exposure measures rely on self-report, introducing recall and reporting bias and potential shared-method variance with self-reported depressive symptoms.
- Depression measurement (CES-D-derived items in Add Health) changes composition across waves, and the manuscript notes the absence of a measurement-invariance or common-item sensitivity analysis in the materials available to it.
- Sibling and monozygotic-twin fixed-effects designs remove stable shared-family (and, for twins, genetic) confounding but cannot remove nonshared, time-varying confounding, and twin designs are additionally limited by small discordant-pair counts and low power.
- The allostatic-load literature is described as subject to measurement heterogeneity, missing-component variability, and threshold dependence, which may explain weaker and non-replicating findings independent of any true biological effect.
- The manuscript is explicit that several of its own results remain provisional pending reconciliation of sample-size discrepancies and reproduction from a final analysis script (see Table 4 of the manuscript, "Submission-critical verification register").

## 6. Unanswered Questions

- Do ambient contextual crime measures and personally reported exposure converge or diverge when modeled together in other cohorts, not only Add Health?
- Are profile-based exposure categories (victimization only, witnessed only, overlap) statistically distinguishable from one another, and does the apparent gradient hold under formal equality testing?
- Does the monozygotic-twin exposure-profile signal reflect a true (if imprecise) association, or would it disappear with a larger discordant-pair sample?
- Is the cum_any_lag prior-exposure-history variable a binary indicator or a dose count, and does that coding choice change the biomarker findings?
- Does the cardiovascular-specific biomarker pattern replicate in cohorts with more complete allostatic-load component data and less missingness?
- Would coefficient magnitudes and significance patterns survive false-discovery-rate correction across the depression, cardiovascular, and multisystem outcome families, which none of the reviewed sources — including the present manuscript's available evidence table — report?

## 7. Synthesis of the Current State of the Field (≈200 words)

The literature cited in this manuscript converges on a claim that is more assumed than tested elsewhere: violence exposure is not one construct but several, distinguished by spatial precision, personal relevance, timing, and the confounding structure each measure is vulnerable to. Prior Add Health work has separately shown that witnessed and direct exposure carry different temporal signatures (Chen et al., 2017), that exposure effects vary by age (Russell et al., 2016), that firearm access and weapon-specific exposure carry depression and cardiovascular risk (Kim, 2018; Ford & Browning, 2014), and that sibling designs can isolate a within-family signal net of shared confounding (Jaffee et al., 2023). Theoretical work in the stress-process, ecological, and life-course traditions supplies the rationale for treating timing and proximity as substantively meaningful rather than as noise (Pearlin, 1989; Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006; Elder, 1998). What remains missing — and what the present manuscript is explicitly built to supply — is a single cohort, single-model comparison of contextual and personal exposure, an integrated exposure-course and weapon-specificity analysis, triangulation across individual, sibling, and monozygotic-twin designs, and a biomarker analysis that separates cardiovascular specificity from broad allostatic-load dysregulation. The field's most consistent unresolved question is whether stronger personal-exposure associations reflect causal proximity or superior measurement — a distinction no cited source, including this manuscript, can yet settle.
