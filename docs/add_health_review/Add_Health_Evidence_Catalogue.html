Synthesis by domain

What the verified Add Health evidence establishes across the nine domains most relevant to the dissertation.

Neighborhood / contextual disadvantage & mental health

Nineteen Add Health studies establish that neighborhood and school socioeconomic disadvantage, perceived hazards, and structural context (including historical redlining) are associated with adolescent depressive symptoms. The recurring methodological finding is that perceived and structural context both matter, but school context often rivals or exceeds neighborhood context (Dunn 2015; Coley 2018; Huang 2020). Crucially, every verified study measures context as perceived hazards, census disadvantage, collective efficacy, or redlining — none links a repeated administrative violent-crime rate to wave-specific depressive symptoms.

Community & street violence exposure

Eight studies link self-reported community/street violence exposure and witnessing to internalizing symptoms and depression, generally using prospective longitudinal regression across Waves I–III. They rely on self-reported exposure rather than objective area crime, and rarely isolate within-person change.

Victimization consequences & fixed-effects/sibling designs

Ten studies form the strongest precedent cluster. Pearson (2025) establishes individual fixed-effects estimation of weapon victimization→depression; Jaffee et al. (2023) establishes cumulative sibling fixed-effects; Kim & Park (2026) adds asymmetric fixed effects; Russell (2016) documents age-varying effects; Turanovic (2019) documents heterogeneity; Semenza (2021) documents victimization trajectories. Together they pre-empt any first-fixed-effects, first-sibling, first-timing, or first-persistence claim — but all use individual self-reported victimization, not administrative area crime.

Moderators & protective factors

Twelve studies test protective/moderating factors — family and parental connectedness (Resnick 1997; Aceves 2006), school connectedness (Henrich 2005; Brookmeyer 2006), religiosity (Nooney 2005; Petts 2008; Fruehwirth 2019, the strongest causal design via peer-religiosity IV), and community involvement (Hull 2008). Buffering hypotheses are well established for self-reported exposure and depression, so moderator novelty must come from applying them to objective contextual crime.

Measurement (CES-D) & causal-inference methods

Thirteen measurement/methods studies anchor causal-inference and CES-D issues: cross-race CES-D non-invariance (Perreira 2005; Harry 2018), contextual-data modeling (Christ 2014), analytic guidelines (Chen & Harris 2020), and modern causal estimators — doubly-robust estimation (Dulce Rubio 2025) and trajectory-model evaluation (Dennis 2025). These supply the identification and measurement scaffolding but do not themselves stake substantive novelty.

Biomarkers & biological embedding

Nine biomarker studies (the HFG extension domain) show social adversity and incarceration predict inflammation/CRP (Boen 2020), gene-regulation disparities (Cole 2020), cardiometabolic risk (Schwartz 2020; Koning 2024), and weathering (Hargrove 2020). CRP from Wave IV dried blood spots is well used. The specific community-violence→CRP design with race×poverty moderation is the biggest unresolved precedent threat (see quarantined CRP dissertation).

Depression / mental health as predictor of later behavior

The reverse pathway — depression predicting later crime — is directly occupied by Anderson, Cesur & Tekin (2015), which finds robust effects concentrated in specific behaviors rather than a broad depression→violence link. Any Paper-3 claim must be framed as extension (distinct outcome or joint exposure), not discovery.

Prediction & machine learning

Three prediction/ML studies (Van Voorhees 2008 CADRA; Geurgas 2025; Ramezani 2026) already predict depression across the life course in Add Health. Algorithmic novelty is weak; defensible value lies in whether validated crime exposure adds temporally-honest, out-of-sample predictive information beyond prior symptoms.

Other Add Health victimization, IPV & health studies

Thirty-two additional Add Health victimization, IPV, sleep, and health studies (largely Tier 2/3) map the broader consequences literature (Turanovic, Warner, Kuhl, Farrell, Fox, Semenza, Testa), confirming saturation of the self-reported victimization→outcome space.

Evidence gaps

Where the verified Add Health literature is silent or thin — the openings the dissertation can defensibly occupy.

  • No administrative area-crime exposure. Across 108 verified Add Health studies, contextual exposure is measured as perceived hazards, census disadvantage, collective efficacy, or redlining — never as a repeated administrative violent-crime rate linked to interview waves. This is the dissertation's clearest open space.
  • Exposure–outcome timing is under-specified. Studies rarely align exposure windows to the CES-D recall period; short pre-interview windows vs. cumulative inter-wave averages are largely untested for contextual crime.
  • Within-person contextual change is unexamined. Individual and sibling fixed effects exist for self-reported victimization (Pearson; Jaffee; Kim & Park) but not for area-level crime exposure.
  • Geographic aggregation. County-level crime may erase local variation; no Add Health study validates a county administrative measure against finer geography for mental-health outcomes.
  • Biomarker precedence is unresolved. A community-violence→CRP design with race×poverty moderation appears to exist (quarantined dissertation) and must be verified before any biomarker novelty claim.
  • Prediction vs. causation are conflated in prior ML work. Incremental, temporally-validated predictive value of crime beyond prior symptoms is not established.

For the proposal / defense

Prior Add Health research has linked self-reported violence and victimization to depression using longitudinal, individual fixed-effects, and sibling-comparison designs (Pearson 2025; Jaffee et al. 2023; Kim & Park 2026), and has characterized neighborhood and school context through perceived hazards, socioeconomic disadvantage, and historical redlining. No verified Add Health study, however, links a repeated administrative county violent-crime measure to within-person depressive change across adolescence and adulthood. This dissertation extends the evidence base by doing so — comparing pre-specified developmental and cumulative-exposure estimands, and evaluating the incremental contribution of contextual crime beyond prior symptoms and established individual risks.
